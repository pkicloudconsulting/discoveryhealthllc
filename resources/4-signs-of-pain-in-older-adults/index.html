4 Signs of Pain in Older Adults That Caregivers Should Not Overlook

Not everyone who hurts says so. Changes in mood, movement, routine, and expression can be the only clues that an older adult is in pain.

By the Discovery Health LLC care team

Not everyone who is in pain talks about it. Some older adults have spent a lifetime pushing through discomfort and do not want to be a bother. Others, particularly those living with dementia or after a stroke, may no longer be able to explain what they feel.

When words are missing, pain finds other ways to show itself. Instead of hearing "my hip hurts" you may notice that your father has gone quiet, that your mother is suddenly refusing her walk, or that meals are being left on the plate. These changes are easy to dismiss as part of growing older. Often they are not. Here are four signs worth a closer look.

1. Changes in mood or behavior

Pain is exhausting, and exhaustion changes personality. Someone who is normally sociable may withdraw from conversation. A patient person may become irritable or impatient. In people with dementia, pain is a common and under-recognized cause of restlessness, agitation, and calling out, especially in the late afternoon and evening.

One change on its own may mean nothing. Several changes appearing together, or a change that lasts more than a few days, deserves attention.

2. Changes in movement

Watch the ordinary transitions: standing up from a chair, getting into bed, walking down the hallway, reaching into a cupboard. Pain shows up as hesitation, as favoring one side, as a hand bracing on furniture, or as a sudden reluctance to do something that used to be routine.

These small differences are often easier to recognize than a verbal complaint, and they are worth describing precisely to a nurse or physician. "She now needs two tries to stand and holds her right knee" is far more useful than "she seems stiffer".

3. Changes in appetite, sleep, and routine

Pain disrupts the basics. Meals get skipped or left half-eaten. Sleep becomes broken, with more waking at night and more dozing in the day. Activities that gave pleasure, such as gardening, church, or a weekly card game, quietly fall away.

Because these changes build slowly, a family member who visits once a week may notice them before the person living with them does. Trust that instinct and write it down.

4. Changes in expression and sound

Facial expressions and sounds are the most direct non-verbal signals of pain. Look for grimacing, frowning, a tightened jaw, or closed eyes during movement or personal care. Listen for moaning, sighing, groaning, or sharp intakes of breath when someone is helped to turn or stand.

Clinicians use structured tools such as the PAINAD scale (Pain Assessment in Advanced Dementia) that score exactly these signs. You do not need the form to use the idea: notice breathing, vocal sounds, facial expression, body language, and how easily the person is consoled.

What to do next

Keep a simple three-day record. Note what you observed, what the person was doing beforehand, how long it lasted, and anything that made it better or worse. Bring that record to the nurse or physician; patterns that are invisible in a ten-minute appointment often jump off a page of notes.

Respond with reassurance rather than persuasion. When someone refuses to walk or skips a meal, pause before encouraging them to push through. Acknowledging that something seems different is more comforting, and more revealing, than coaxing them to continue with an activity that hurts.

And remember that pain is not a normal part of aging. It is a symptom, and most causes can be treated or eased once they are recognized.

How a consistent care team helps

One of the quiet advantages of regular home care is familiarity. A nurse or caregiver who visits the same person week after week learns what normal looks like for them, and notices when it shifts. Discovery Health LLC monitors vital signs and health status, watches for changes in condition, documents what we see, and communicates it to your physician and family so that pain is addressed early rather than endured.
